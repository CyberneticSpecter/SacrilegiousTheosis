## 2.1 Epistemology

To answer "what to do?", we need knowledge about the world and our place in it. This raises the question - "how do we know stuff?" 

This leads us to "epistemology" [1].
### 2.1.1 Appearances

Now, in attempting to investigate our candidate means of knowledge, it seems that our primary means of conscious contact with the world (and, by extension, knowledge of it) is through "appearances."

The world "appears" to us in a certain way. We, by and large, rely on these appearances to navigate the world and infer its nature. 

Although appearances can be deceiving (as the adage goes), even the clues of such deceit themselves are generally still found in appearances. 

For example, given an array of appearances, we may make our best attempts at theorizing the underlying world that generates those appearances. In doing so, we might find reasons to suspect that certain aspects of appearances are systematically misleading (at their first glance) - such as the popular example of a straight stick appearing bent in the water.  

Other times, testimony by others on what "appeared" to them may provide clues of deceit.  Thus such a knowledge of deceit also indirectly comes from appearance. These points will be explored more in depth later on, but we provide some initial gist here to motivate the exploration on the nature of appearances.

Either way, by and large, it seems that appearances (directly or indirectly, subjectively or intersubjectivity) serve as foundations for our knowledge base despite them not being a perfect source of knowledge all the time. 

Thus, to better understand the world and our means to know about the world, it may be prudent to first investigate, more deeply, on the nature of "appearances." 
### 2.1.1.1 Specification of Appearance

What do I specifically mean by "appearance?" (I may use terms like "seeming" interchangeably with "appearance")

I believe, I am using the term in a fairly standard sense, but it is still better to be clearer by using examples and contrasting uses. 

I am using "appearance" as a broad notion that encompasses: 
* Perceptual appearance (which includes illusions, hallucinations, and veridical perceptions)
	* The visual appearance of a computer (on which I am typing) in front of me is an example of what is perceptually appearing to me now.
* Intellectual appearance (or what philosophers call "intuitions")
	*  2+2=4 can be an intellectual (intuitive) appearance when I am actively thinking about it and feel compelled to assent to the proposition.
* Introspective or interoceptive appearance
	* The feeling of hunger can be an example of an interoceptive appearance. 
* Mnemonic appearance 
	*  The recalling that yesterday, I ate a whole chocolate bar can be an example of a mnemonic appearance (appearance from memory).

(there could be possibly other types of appearances that do not immediately come to my mind as of now).

Under the sense in which I am using the notion of "appearance", note that:

1. An appearance of P is not a belief in P
2. An appearance of P is not a disposition to believe in P
3. An appearance of P is not a disposition to believe that there is evidence for P
4. An appearance of P is not necessarily an experience.
5. Appearance is not necessarily Acquaintance.
